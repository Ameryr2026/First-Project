Energy estimates and a priori bounds

The idea

An a priori estimate is a bound that every smooth solution must satisfy, proved before knowing that solutions exist. The standard way to find one: multiply the equation by a well-chosen quantity, integrate, integrate by parts, and use the structure of the equation so that the dangerous terms cancel or have a sign. The choice of multiplier is the idea; the rest is the inequality toolkit.

Statement

The method (not a theorem).

  1. Multiply each equation by a multiplier (often the unknown itself, its time derivative, or $\Delta$ of it) and integrate over $\Omega$.
  2. Integrate by parts, using the boundary conditions, to produce $\frac{d}{dt}$(energy) plus nonnegative dissipation.
  3. Arrange the multipliers so that coupling terms cancel between equations.
  4. Bound what remains by the energy and dissipation (Hölder, Sobolev, Young), absorb the dissipation parts, and close with Gronwall.
  5. For higher regularity, repeat after differentiating the equation in $t$ or $x$.

Model example (heat equation). For $u_t-\Delta u=0$ with $u=0$ on $\partial\Omega$, multiplying by $u$ gives $\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\norm{u}_{L^2}^2+\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2=0$, and multiplying by $u_t$ gives $\norm{u_t}_{L^2}^2+\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2=0$.

Why it is plausible

Physical systems usually dissipate energy, and the mathematical energy is the physical one. Kinetic energy, internal (pressure) energy and potential energy together cannot grow; each piece then bounds a norm of the unknowns. When the energy contains a term with no sign, the first job is to show it is still bounded below, typically by interpolation against a conserved quantity.

Where to find a proof

  • L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, §2.3.4 and §7.1.2 (energy methods for the heat equation and for linear parabolic equations).
