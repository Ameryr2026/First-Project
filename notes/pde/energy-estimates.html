Energy estimates and a priori bounds

The idea

An a priori estimate is a bound that every smooth solution must satisfy, proved before knowing that solutions exist. The standard way to find one: multiply the equation by a well-chosen quantity, integrate, integrate by parts, and use the structure of the equation so that the dangerous terms cancel or have a sign. The choice of multiplier is the idea; the rest is the inequality toolkit.

Statement

The method (not a theorem).

  1. Multiply each equation by a multiplier (often the unknown itself, its time derivative, or $\Delta$ of it) and integrate over $\Omega$. How to choose it is explained under Choosing the multiplier below.
  2. Integrate by parts, using the boundary conditions, to produce $\frac{d}{dt}$(energy) plus nonnegative dissipation.
  3. Arrange the multipliers so that coupling terms cancel between equations.
  4. Bound what remains by the energy and dissipation (Hölder, Sobolev, Young), absorb the dissipation parts, and close with Gronwall.
  5. For higher regularity, repeat after differentiating the equation in $t$ or $x$.

Model example (heat equation). For $u_t-\Delta u=0$ with $u=0$ on $\partial\Omega$, multiplying by $u$ gives $\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\norm{u}_{L^2}^2+\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2=0$, and multiplying by $u_t$ gives $\norm{u_t}_{L^2}^2+\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2=0$.

Choosing the multiplier

The multiplier is not guessed. First decide which quantity you want to bound; the multiplier is its derivative.

The multiplier rule. To control $\int_\Omega \Phi(u)\,dx$, multiply the equation by $\Phi'(u)$. By the chain rule the time term becomes exactly the time derivative of that quantity: $$\int_\Omega \Phi'(u)\,u_t\,dx=\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega \Phi(u)\,dx.$$ For the diffusion term, integration by parts gives $$-\int_\Omega \Delta u\,\Phi'(u)\,dx=\int_\Omega \Phi''(u)\,\abs{\nabla u}^2\,dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Phi'(u)\,\partial_\nu u\,dS .$$ The boundary term vanishes under Neumann conditions ($\partial_\nu u=0$), or under Dirichlet conditions ($u=0$) when $\Phi'(0)=0$. If $\Phi$ is convex, $\Phi''\ge 0$, so the remaining term is nonnegative: it is dissipation, which can be kept or dropped.

The standard choices for $u_t-\Delta u=f$:

  • $\Phi(s)=\frac12 s^2$, multiplier $u$. Dissipation $\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2$. This is the basic energy estimate.
  • $\Phi(s)=\frac1p\abs{s}^p$ ($p\ge 2$), multiplier $\abs{u}^{p-2}u$. Dissipation $(p-1)\int\abs{u}^{p-2}\abs{\nabla u}^2$. Controls $\norm{u}_{L^p}$.
  • $\Phi(s)=s\log s$ (for $u>0$), multiplier $\log u+1$. Dissipation $\int \abs{\nabla u}^2/u$. This is the entropy, the standard functional for chemotaxis (Keller–Segel) models.
  • $\Phi(s)=\abs{s}$, multiplier $\operatorname{sign}(u)$. Controls $\norm{u}_{L^1}$, natural for densities and mass. Since $\abs{s}$ is not differentiable at $0$, it has to be smoothed first (next example).

The smoothed sign and the $L^1$ estimate. Take $u_t-\Delta u=f$ with $u=0$ on $\partial\Omega$. Replace $\abs{s}$ by the smooth convex function $$\Phi_\delta(s)=\sqrt{s^2+\delta^2},\qquad \Phi_\delta'(s)=\frac{s}{\sqrt{s^2+\delta^2}}\;\longrightarrow\;\operatorname{sign}(s),\qquad \Phi_\delta''(s)=\frac{\delta^2}{(s^2+\delta^2)^{3/2}}\ge 0 .$$ Since $\Phi_\delta'(0)=0$, the boundary term vanishes, and multiplying by $\Phi_\delta'(u)$ gives $$\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega \Phi_\delta(u)\,dx+\int_\Omega \Phi_\delta''(u)\abs{\nabla u}^2\,dx=\int_\Omega f\,\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^2+\delta^2}}\,dx\le \norm{f}_{L^1}.$$ Drop the nonnegative term and integrate in time. Since $\abs{s}\le\Phi_\delta(s)\le\abs{s}+\delta$, letting $\delta\to 0$ gives $$\norm{u(t)}_{L^1}\le \norm{u_0}_{L^1}+\int_0^t\norm{f(s)}_{L^1}\,ds .$$ Applied to the difference of two solutions of a linear problem, the same computation gives $L^1$ contraction, and hence uniqueness.

Multipliers with derivatives. The same rule applies when the quantity you want involves derivatives of $u$:

  • To control $\frac12\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2$: its time derivative is $\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla u_t=\int(-\Delta u)\,u_t$, so the multiplier is $-\Delta u$. Equivalently, apply $\partial_i$ to the equation and multiply by $\partial_i u$: this is the basic estimate for $\nabla u$.
  • To control $\frac12\norm{\Delta u}_{L^2}^2$: the multiplier is $\Delta^2 u$.
  • The multiplier $u_t$ gives $\norm{u_t}_{L^2}^2+\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2$. It controls the time derivative, which is what compactness (Aubin–Lions) needs.

Reading off the function spaces. Every estimate above has the form $$E(t)+\int_0^t D(s)\,ds\le E(0)\quad(\text{plus data terms}),$$ with $E\ge 0$ the energy and $D\ge 0$ the dissipation. Each term of $E$ is bounded at every time, so it gives a space that is $L^\infty$ in time. Each term of $D$ is bounded only after integrating in time; since it appears squared, it gives a space that is $L^2$ in time. For the heat equation, the estimate at level $s$ (multiplier $u$, $-\Delta u$, $\Delta^2u$, …) gives $$u\in L^\infty(0,T;H^s)\cap L^2(0,T;H^{s+1}).$$ The level is chosen by the nonlinearity, not by the estimate: you go up until the nonlinear terms can be bounded, typically until $s>d/2$, where $H^s\subset L^\infty$.

Why it is plausible

Physical systems usually dissipate energy, and the mathematical energy is the physical one. Kinetic energy, internal (pressure) energy and potential energy together cannot grow; each piece then bounds a norm of the unknowns. When the energy contains a term with no sign, the first job is to show it is still bounded below, typically by interpolation against a conserved quantity.

Exercises

Exercise 1. Derive the two identities for the heat equation in the model example, with every integration by parts and boundary term. Conclude that $u_0 \in H^1_0$ gives $u \in L^\infty(0,T;H^1_0)$ and $u_t \in L^2(0,T;L^2)$.

Hint

$\int u_t u = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2$ and $-\int\Delta u\,u = \norm{\nabla u}^2$ (boundary term vanishes since $u = 0$). Similarly $-\int\Delta u\,u_t = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\nabla u}^2$ because $u_t = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$. Integrate in time.

Solution

Multiplying by $u$: $\int u_tu = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2$ and $-\int\Delta u\,u = \int|\nabla u|^2 - \int_{\partial\Omega}u\,\partial_\nu u = \norm{\nabla u}^2$ since $u = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$. So $\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2 + \norm{\nabla u}^2 = 0$.

Multiplying by $u_t$: $-\int\Delta u\,u_t = \int\nabla u\cdot\nabla u_t - \int_{\partial\Omega}u_t\,\partial_\nu u = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\nabla u}^2$, because $u_t = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$ ($u$ vanishes there for all $t$). So $\norm{u_t}^2 + \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\nabla u}^2 = 0$.

Integrating: $\norm{u(t)}^2 \le \norm{u_0}^2$ and $\norm{\nabla u(t)}^2 + 2\int_0^t\norm{u_t}^2 = \norm{\nabla u_0}^2$. So $u \in L^\infty(0,T;H^1_0)$ and $u_t \in L^2(0,T;L^2)$ when $u_0 \in H^1_0$.

Exercise 2. A nonlinear term with a sign. For $u_t - \Delta u = u - u^3$ with $\partial_\nu u = 0$, multiply by $u$ and show $\norm{u(t)}_{L^2}^2 \le \norm{u_0}_{L^2}^2 + \frac{|\Omega|}{2}t$.

Hint

$\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2 + \norm{\nabla u}^2 = \int(u^2 - u^4)$, and $s^2 - s^4 \le \frac14$ for all real $s$.

Solution

Multiplying by $u$ and using $\partial_\nu u = 0$: $\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2 + \norm{\nabla u}^2 = \int_\Omega(u^2 - u^4)$. Since $s^2 - s^4 = \frac14 - (s^2 - \frac12)^2 \le \frac14$, the right side is at most $\frac{|\Omega|}{4}$. Dropping $\norm{\nabla u}^2 \ge 0$, $\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}^2 \le \frac{|\Omega|}{2}$, and integrating, $\norm{u(t)}^2 \le \norm{u_0}^2 + \frac{|\Omega|}{2}t$. The cubic term was not estimated at all: its sign did the work.

Exercise 3. Choosing the multiplier. For the wave equation $u_{tt} = u_{xx}$ on $(0,1)$ with $u = 0$ at both ends, find a multiplier that shows $\int_0^1 (u_t^2 + u_x^2)\,dx$ is constant in time.

Hint

Multiply by $u_t$: $u_{tt}u_t = \frac12(u_t^2)_t$ and $\int u_{xx}u_t = -\int u_x u_{xt} = -\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\int u_x^2$, since $u_t = 0$ at the ends.

Solution

Multiply by $u_t$ and integrate over $(0,1)$. First, $\int u_{tt}u_t = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t^2$. Second, $\int u_{xx}u_t = \big[u_xu_t\big]_0^1 - \int u_xu_{xt} = -\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\int u_x^2$, since $u_t = 0$ at $x = 0, 1$. So $\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^1(u_t^2 + u_x^2)\,dx = 0$.

Exercise 4. The $L^p$ estimate. For $u_t - \Delta u = 0$ with $u = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$ and $p \ge 2$, show that $\norm{u(t)}_{L^p} \le \norm{u_0}_{L^p}$ for all $t \ge 0$.

Hint

Use the multiplier rule with $\Phi(s) = \frac1p\abs{s}^p$. Check that $\Phi'(0) = 0$ and $\Phi'' \ge 0$.

Solution

Multiply by $\Phi'(u) = \abs{u}^{p-2}u$, which vanishes on $\partial\Omega$. The time term is $\int \abs{u}^{p-2}u\,u_t = \frac1p\frac{d}{dt}\int\abs{u}^p$. The diffusion term is $-\int \Delta u\,\abs{u}^{p-2}u = \int \nabla u\cdot\nabla(\abs{u}^{p-2}u) = (p-1)\int\abs{u}^{p-2}\abs{\nabla u}^2 \ge 0$, using $\Phi''(s) = (p-1)\abs{s}^{p-2}$ (continuous since $p \ge 2$). So $\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u}_{L^p}^p \le 0$, and integrating, $\norm{u(t)}_{L^p}^p \le \norm{u_0}_{L^p}^p$.

Exercise 5. A higher-order estimate. On the torus $\mathbb{T}^d$ (periodic boundary conditions, so integration by parts has no boundary terms), let $u_t - \Delta u = f$. Multiply by $\Delta^2 u$ and show $$\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\Delta u}_{L^2}^2 + \norm{\nabla\Delta u}_{L^2}^2 \le \norm{\nabla f}_{L^2}^2 .$$ Which spaces does this put $\Delta u$ and $\nabla\Delta u$ in?

Hint

Integrate by parts twice in the time term and once in the other two. Finish with Cauchy–Schwarz and Young: $ab \le \frac12a^2 + \frac12b^2$.

Solution

Time term: $\int u_t\,\Delta^2u = \int \Delta u_t\,\Delta u = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\Delta u}^2$. Diffusion term: $-\int\Delta u\,\Delta^2u = \int\nabla\Delta u\cdot\nabla\Delta u = \norm{\nabla\Delta u}^2$. Forcing: $\int f\,\Delta^2u = -\int\nabla f\cdot\nabla\Delta u \le \norm{\nabla f}\norm{\nabla\Delta u} \le \frac12\norm{\nabla f}^2 + \frac12\norm{\nabla\Delta u}^2$. Absorbing the last term into the left side and multiplying by $2$ gives the inequality.

Integrating in time, $\Delta u \in L^\infty(0,T;L^2)$ and $\nabla\Delta u \in L^2(0,T;L^2)$ whenever $\Delta u_0 \in L^2$ and $\nabla f \in L^2(0,T;L^2)$: the level-2 spaces $L^\infty(0,T;H^2)\cap L^2(0,T;H^3)$, up to lower-order terms.
