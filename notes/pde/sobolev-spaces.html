Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces

The idea

PDE solutions are rarely smooth enough for classical derivatives, but they can be integrated against smooth test functions. A weak derivative is defined by the integration-by-parts formula, and Sobolev spaces measure how many weak derivatives a function has and in which $L^p$ space they lie. Almost every estimate in PDE analysis is a bound on a Sobolev norm.

Statement

Definition. Let $\Omega \subset \R^n$ be open and $u, v \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$. Then $v = \partial^\alpha u$ weakly if $\int_\Omega u\, \partial^\alpha \varphi \,dx = (-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_\Omega v\,\varphi\,dx$ for all $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. For $k \in \N$ and $1 \le p \le \infty$,

\[ W^{k,p}(\Omega) = \{ u \in L^p(\Omega) : \partial^\alpha u \in L^p(\Omega) \text{ for all } |\alpha| \le k \}, \qquad \norm{u}_{W^{k,p}} = \sum_{|\alpha|\le k} \norm{\partial^\alpha u}_{L^p}. \]

$H^k = W^{k,2}$, and $H^1_0(\Omega)$ is the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ in $H^1(\Omega)$: the $H^1$ functions that vanish on $\partial\Omega$ in the trace sense.

The dual space $H^{-1}$. $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is the dual of $H^1_0(\Omega)$: the space of bounded linear functionals $f$ on $H^1_0(\Omega)$. Write $\langle f, v\rangle$ for the value of $f$ at $v$; this is the pairing between $H^{-1}$ and $H^1_0$. The norm is

\[ \norm{f}_{H^{-1}} = \sup\big\{ \langle f, v\rangle : v \in H^1_0(\Omega),\ \norm{v}_{H^1} \le 1 \big\}. \]

Each $f \in L^2(\Omega)$ defines an element of $H^{-1}$ by $\langle f, v\rangle = \int_\Omega f v\,dx$, so $H^1_0 \subset L^2 \subset H^{-1}$, each space continuously embedded in the next; for $f \in L^2$ the pairing is the $L^2$ inner product. Here $H^1_0$ is not identified with its own dual: $L^2$ is the space in the middle.

Theorem (what elements of $H^{-1}$ look like). $f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ if and only if there are $f^0, f^1, \dots, f^n \in L^2(\Omega)$ with $\langle f, v\rangle = \int_\Omega \big(f^0 v + \sum_{i=1}^n f^i \partial_i v\big)\,dx$ for all $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$. In the sense of distributions, $f = f^0 - \sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i f^i$. Moreover $\norm{f}_{H^{-1}}$ is the infimum of $\big(\int_\Omega \sum_{i=0}^n |f^i|^2\,dx\big)^{1/2}$ over all such representations.

Homogeneous spaces. $D^{k,r}(\Omega) = \{ u \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}} : \norm{\nabla^k u}_{L^r} \lt \infty \}$ with seminorm $\norm{\nabla^k u}_{L^r}$, and $D^k = D^{k,2}$. These record only the top derivative. They are convenient for quantities like the velocity, where the estimates naturally control $\nabla u$ and $\nabla^2 u$ but not $u$ itself.

Facts to know. $W^{k,p}$ is a Banach space; $H^k$ is a Hilbert space; for $1 \le p \lt \infty$ smooth functions are dense in $W^{k,p}(\Omega)$ when $\partial\Omega$ is Lipschitz; and for $1 \lt p \lt \infty$ the space is reflexive.

Why it is plausible

The definition is forced: it is the only one that agrees with the classical derivative for smooth $u$ and makes sense when $u$ is merely integrable. A function like $|x|$ on $(-1,1)$ has weak derivative $\operatorname{sign}(x)$, but $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ has no weak derivative in $L^1$: a jump is the first thing weak differentiation cannot absorb.

$H^{-1}$ is where one derivative of an $L^2$ function lives: $H^1_0$ functions have one derivative more than $L^2$, and elements of $H^{-1}$ have one derivative less. For example, if $u \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ then $\Delta u \in H^{-1}$, since $\langle \Delta u, v\rangle = -\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,dx$ is bounded by $\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{H^1}$. So for the heat equation $u_t = \Delta u$ with $u \in L^2(0,T;H^1_0)$, the time derivative $u_t$ lies in $L^2(0,T;H^{-1})$: exactly the setting of the continuity theorem on spaces of time-dependent functions. In one dimension the point evaluation $v \mapsto v(0)$ belongs to $H^{-1}(-1,1)$, because $H^1$ functions are continuous there; in two or more dimensions it does not.

Exercises

Exercise 1. Let $u(x) = |x|$ on $(-1,1)$. Show directly from the definition that its weak derivative is $\operatorname{sign}(x)$. Then show that $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ has no weak derivative in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(-1,1)$.

Hint

For the first part, split $\int_{-1}^1 |x|\varphi'(x)\,dx$ at $0$ and integrate by parts on each side; the boundary terms at $0$ cancel. For the second, compute $\int_{-1}^1 \operatorname{sign}(x)\varphi'(x)\,dx = -2\varphi(0)$. If $v$ were a weak derivative, testing with $\varphi$ supported away from $0$ forces $v = 0$ a.e., and then $-2\varphi(0) = 0$ for every $\varphi$, which is false.

Solution

Let $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(-1,1)$. Splitting at $0$ and integrating by parts on each side,

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{-1}^0 (-x)\varphi'\,dx &= \big[-x\varphi\big]_{-1}^0 + \int_{-1}^0 \varphi\,dx = \int_{-1}^0\varphi\,dx, \\ \int_0^1 x\varphi'\,dx &= \big[x\varphi\big]_0^1 - \int_0^1\varphi\,dx = -\int_0^1\varphi\,dx, \end{aligned} \]

since $\varphi(\pm1) = 0$. Adding, $\int_{-1}^1|x|\varphi'\,dx = -\int_{-1}^1\operatorname{sign}(x)\varphi\,dx$, which is the definition of $|x|' = \operatorname{sign}(x)$ weakly.

Next, $\int_{-1}^1\operatorname{sign}(x)\varphi'\,dx = -\int_{-1}^0\varphi' + \int_0^1\varphi' = -\varphi(0) - \varphi(0) = -2\varphi(0)$. Suppose $v \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ were the weak derivative, so $\int\operatorname{sign}(x)\varphi' = -\int v\varphi$ for all $\varphi$. For $\varphi$ supported in $(0,1)$ the left side is $\int_0^1\varphi' = 0$, so $\int v\varphi = 0$ for all such $\varphi$ and $v = 0$ a.e. on $(0,1)$; likewise on $(-1,0)$. Then $-2\varphi(0) = 0$ for every test function, which fails for any $\varphi$ with $\varphi(0) = 1$.

Exercise 2. Let $B$ be the unit ball in $\R^n$, $1 \le p \lt n$, and $u(x) = |x|^{-s}$ with $s \gt 0$. Show that $u \in W^{1,p}(B)$ if and only if $s \lt \frac{n-p}{p}$. Conclude that in $\R^3$ there are unbounded functions in $H^1(B)$.

Hint

Away from $0$, $|\nabla u| = s|x|^{-s-1}$, and in polar coordinates $\int_B |x|^{-(s+1)p}\,dx = c\int_0^1 r^{n-1-(s+1)p}\,dr$, finite exactly when $(s+1)p \lt n$. To see that the pointwise gradient is the weak gradient, integrate by parts on $B\setminus B_\varepsilon$: the boundary term is at most $C\varepsilon^{n-1-s}$, which tends to $0$ because $s \lt \frac{n-p}{p} \le n-1$. In $\R^3$ with $p = 2$, any $s \in (0,\frac12)$ works.

Solution

In polar coordinates, with $\omega_{n-1}$ the area of the unit sphere,

\[ \int_B |x|^{-sp}\,dx = \omega_{n-1}\int_0^1 r^{n-1-sp}\,dr, \qquad \int_B |\nabla u|^p\,dx = s^p\omega_{n-1}\int_0^1 r^{n-1-(s+1)p}\,dr, \]

using $\nabla u = -s|x|^{-s-2}x$ and $|\nabla u| = s|x|^{-s-1}$ away from $0$. The first is finite iff $sp \lt n$, the second iff $(s+1)p \lt n$, i.e. $s \lt \frac{n-p}{p}$; the second condition implies the first.

It remains to check that the pointwise gradient is the weak gradient when $s \lt \frac{n-p}{p}$. For $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(B)$ and $\varepsilon \gt 0$, the divergence theorem on $B\setminus B_\varepsilon$ gives

\[ \int_{B\setminus B_\varepsilon} u\,\partial_i\varphi\,dx = -\int_{B\setminus B_\varepsilon}\partial_iu\,\varphi\,dx + \int_{\partial B_\varepsilon} u\,\varphi\,\nu_i\,dS, \]

with $\nu$ the normal on the small sphere. The last term is at most $\norm{\varphi}_{L^\infty}\varepsilon^{-s}\,\omega_{n-1}\varepsilon^{n-1}$, which tends to $0$ because $s \lt \frac{n-p}{p} = \frac np - 1 \le n-1$. Since $u, \partial_iu \in L^p(B) \subset L^1(B)$, letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives $\int_B u\,\partial_i\varphi = -\int_B \partial_iu\,\varphi$. Conversely, if $s \ge \frac{n-p}{p}$, a weak gradient would agree a.e. with the classical one on $B\setminus\{0\}$, which is not in $L^p$.

For $n = 3$, $p = 2$: $\frac{n-p}{p} = \frac12$, so $|x|^{-s} \in H^1(B)$ for every $s \in (0,\frac12)$, and these functions are unbounded near $0$.

Exercise 3. (a) Show that every $f \in L^2(\Omega)$ satisfies $\norm{f}_{H^{-1}} \le \norm{f}_{L^2}$. (b) On $\Omega = (-1,1)$, show that $\delta: v \mapsto v(0)$ belongs to $H^{-1}(\Omega)$. (c) Write $\delta = f^0 - (f^1)'$ with $f^0, f^1 \in L^2(-1,1)$.

Hint

(a) $|\int f v| \le \norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{L^2} \le \norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{H^1}$. (b) For $v \in H^1_0(-1,1)$, $v(0) = \int_{-1}^0 v'(x)\,dx$, so $|v(0)| \le \norm{v'}_{L^2} \le \norm{v}_{H^1}$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. (c) The formula in (b) says $\langle \delta, v\rangle = \int f^1 v'$ with $f^1 = \mathbf{1}_{(-1,0)}$ and $f^0 = 0$.

Solution

(a) For $v \in H^1_0$, $|\langle f,v\rangle| = |\int fv| \le \norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{L^2} \le \norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{H^1}$. Taking the supremum over $\norm{v}_{H^1} \le 1$ gives $\norm{f}_{H^{-1}} \le \norm{f}_{L^2}$.

(b) For $v \in C_c^\infty(-1,1)$, $v(0) = v(0) - v(-1) = \int_{-1}^0 v'\,dx$, so by Cauchy–Schwarz $|v(0)| \le 1\cdot\norm{v'}_{L^2(-1,0)} \le \norm{v}_{H^1}$. The same bound, applied to differences, shows $\sup|v| \le \sqrt2\,\norm{v'}_{L^2}$, so $H^1_0$-limits of test functions converge uniformly; hence every $v \in H^1_0(-1,1)$ is continuous, $v(0)$ makes sense, and $|v(0)| \le \norm{v}_{H^1}$ still holds. So $\delta$ is a bounded linear functional: $\delta \in H^{-1}$ with $\norm{\delta}_{H^{-1}} \le 1$.

(c) The formula in (b) reads $\langle\delta, v\rangle = \int_{-1}^1 f^1 v'\,dx$ with $f^1 = \mathbf{1}_{(-1,0)}$ and $f^0 = 0$. In the form of the theorem, $\delta = f^0 - (f^1)'$; indeed, for test functions, $\langle -(f^1)', \varphi\rangle = \int f^1\varphi' = \varphi(0) - \varphi(-1) = \varphi(0)$.
