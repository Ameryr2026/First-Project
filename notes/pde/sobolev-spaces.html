Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces

The idea

PDE solutions are rarely smooth enough for classical derivatives, but they can be integrated against smooth test functions. A weak derivative is defined by the integration-by-parts formula, and Sobolev spaces measure how many weak derivatives a function has and in which $L^p$ space they lie. Almost every estimate in PDE analysis is a bound on a Sobolev norm.

Statement

Definition. Let $\Omega \subset \R^n$ be open and $u, v \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$. Then $v = \partial^\alpha u$ weakly if $\int_\Omega u\, \partial^\alpha \varphi \,dx = (-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_\Omega v\,\varphi\,dx$ for all $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. For $k \in \N$ and $1 \le p \le \infty$,

\[ W^{k,p}(\Omega) = \{ u \in L^p(\Omega) : \partial^\alpha u \in L^p(\Omega) \text{ for all } |\alpha| \le k \}, \qquad \norm{u}_{W^{k,p}} = \sum_{|\alpha|\le k} \norm{\partial^\alpha u}_{L^p}. \]

$H^k = W^{k,2}$, and $H^1_0(\Omega)$ is the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ in $H^1(\Omega)$: the $H^1$ functions that vanish on $\partial\Omega$ in the trace sense.

The dual space $H^{-1}$. $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is the dual of $H^1_0(\Omega)$: the space of bounded linear functionals $f$ on $H^1_0(\Omega)$. Write $\langle f, v\rangle$ for the value of $f$ at $v$; this is the pairing between $H^{-1}$ and $H^1_0$. The norm is

\[ \norm{f}_{H^{-1}} = \sup\big\{ \langle f, v\rangle : v \in H^1_0(\Omega),\ \norm{v}_{H^1} \le 1 \big\}. \]

Each $f \in L^2(\Omega)$ defines an element of $H^{-1}$ by $\langle f, v\rangle = \int_\Omega f v\,dx$, so $H^1_0 \subset L^2 \subset H^{-1}$, each space continuously embedded in the next; for $f \in L^2$ the pairing is the $L^2$ inner product. Here $H^1_0$ is not identified with its own dual: $L^2$ is the space in the middle.

Theorem (what elements of $H^{-1}$ look like). $f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ if and only if there are $f^0, f^1, \dots, f^n \in L^2(\Omega)$ with $\langle f, v\rangle = \int_\Omega \big(f^0 v + \sum_{i=1}^n f^i \partial_i v\big)\,dx$ for all $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$. In the sense of distributions, $f = f^0 - \sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i f^i$. Moreover $\norm{f}_{H^{-1}}$ is the infimum of $\big(\int_\Omega \sum_{i=0}^n |f^i|^2\,dx\big)^{1/2}$ over all such representations.

Homogeneous spaces. $D^{k,r}(\Omega) = \{ u \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}} : \norm{\nabla^k u}_{L^r} \lt \infty \}$ with seminorm $\norm{\nabla^k u}_{L^r}$, and $D^k = D^{k,2}$. These record only the top derivative. They are convenient for quantities like the velocity, where the estimates naturally control $\nabla u$ and $\nabla^2 u$ but not $u$ itself.

Facts to know. $W^{k,p}$ is a Banach space; $H^k$ is a Hilbert space; for $1 \le p \lt \infty$ smooth functions are dense in $W^{k,p}(\Omega)$ when $\partial\Omega$ is Lipschitz; and for $1 \lt p \lt \infty$ the space is reflexive.

Why it is plausible

The definition is forced: it is the only one that agrees with the classical derivative for smooth $u$ and makes sense when $u$ is merely integrable. A function like $|x|$ on $(-1,1)$ has weak derivative $\operatorname{sign}(x)$, but $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ has no weak derivative in $L^1$: a jump is the first thing weak differentiation cannot absorb.

$H^{-1}$ is where one derivative of an $L^2$ function lives: $H^1_0$ functions have one derivative more than $L^2$, and elements of $H^{-1}$ have one derivative less. For example, if $u \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ then $\Delta u \in H^{-1}$, since $\langle \Delta u, v\rangle = -\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,dx$ is bounded by $\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{H^1}$. So for the heat equation $u_t = \Delta u$ with $u \in L^2(0,T;H^1_0)$, the time derivative $u_t$ lies in $L^2(0,T;H^{-1})$: exactly the setting of the continuity theorem on spaces of time-dependent functions. In one dimension the point evaluation $v \mapsto v(0)$ belongs to $H^{-1}(-1,1)$, because $H^1$ functions are continuous there; in two or more dimensions it does not.
