Chemotaxis compressible Navier–Stokes equations

Based on T. Huang, B. Rababah, A. Rawashdeh, On existence of chemotaxis compressible Navier–Stokes equations modeling vascular network formation, J. Differential Equations 480 (2026) 114655. doi:10.1016/j.jde.2026.114655

This page walks through a complete existence theory for one nonlinear system. The aim is to show how the standard tools of PDE analysis fit together in a real argument. Every tool is linked to its own concept page, which states it precisely and points to a proof. Steps are tagged routine when a standard tool is used in the standard way, and new idea when the argument does something that cannot be taken off the shelf.

1. The model

Endothelial cells, spread on a substrate, organize themselves into a network of blood vessels. They move like a compressible fluid and are attracted up the gradient of a chemical that they themselves secrete. In a bounded domain $\Omega \subset \R^3$ with smooth boundary, the unknowns are the cell density $\rho \ge 0$, the cell velocity $u \in \R^3$, and the chemoattractant concentration $\phi \ge 0$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:system} \left\{ \begin{aligned} &\rho_t + \dv(\rho u) = 0, \\ &(\rho u)_t + \dv(\rho u \otimes u) + \nabla p(\rho) - \mu \Delta u - (\lambda+\mu)\nabla \dv u = \chi \rho \nabla \phi - \alpha \rho u, \\ &\sigma \phi_t = D \Delta \phi + a \rho - b \phi, \end{aligned} \right. \end{equation}

with pressure $p(\rho) = \frac{\theta(\gamma-1)}{\gamma}\rho^\gamma$, $\gamma > 1$. The coefficients satisfy $D, \chi, \alpha, a, \theta > 0$, $\mu > 0$, $3\lambda + 2\mu > 0$, and $b, \sigma \ge 0$.

  • $\chi \rho \nabla \phi$ is the chemotactic force: cells are pushed up the gradient of $\phi$.
  • $\alpha \rho u$ is friction with the substrate (damping).
  • $a\rho$ is secretion of the chemical by the cells; $b\phi$ is its decay.
  • The pressure models the fact that closely packed cells resist compression.

The boundary and initial conditions are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc} u = 0, \quad \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \nu} = 0 \ \text{ on } \partial\Omega, \qquad (\rho, u, \phi)|_{t=0} = (\rho_0, u_0, \phi_0). \end{equation}

Setting $\mu = \lambda = 0$ gives the inviscid hyperbolic–parabolic model of Gamba et al. (2003), which reproduces the network patterns seen in experiments. Here we keep the viscosity.

Two difficulties to keep in mind

Vacuum. The density may vanish on an open set. Where $\rho = 0$ the momentum equation degenerates: the term $\rho u_t$ gives no control of $u_t$. Both theorems below allow this.

An energy with no sign. The natural energy contains the cross term $-\chi\rho\phi$, which can be negative and large. Before any estimate can be used, one must show that the energy is bounded below.

2. The main results

Theorem A (local strong solutions). Let $q \in (3,6]$. Suppose $\rho_0 \ge 0$, $\rho_0 \in W^{1,q}$, $u_0 \in D^2 \cap D^1_0$, $0 \le \phi_0 \in H^2$, and the compatibility condition

\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat} \mu \Delta u_0 + (\lambda+\mu)\nabla \dv u_0 - \nabla p(\rho_0) + \chi \rho_0 \nabla \phi_0 - \alpha \rho_0 u_0 = \sqrt{\rho_0}\, g \quad \text{for some } g \in L^2(\Omega) \end{equation}

holds. Then there are $T_0 > 0$ and a unique strong solution of \eqref{eq:system}–\eqref{eq:bc} on $\Omega \times (0, T_0]$.

Proof of Theorem A: to be written. The map in Section 4 lists the steps in order.

Theorem B (global weak solutions). Let $\gamma > 3/2$. Suppose $0 \le \rho_0 \in L^\gamma$ with $\rho_0 \not\equiv 0$, $\sqrt{\rho_0}\, u_0 \in L^2$, and $0 \le \phi_0 \in W^{2 - 2/\gamma,\, \gamma}$. Then for every $T > 0$ there is a finite-energy weak solution of \eqref{eq:system}–\eqref{eq:bc} on $\Omega \times (0,T)$.

Proof of Theorem B: to be written. The map in Section 5 lists the steps in order.

What "strong solution" and "finite-energy weak solution" mean, with the exact function spaces, is on Strong, weak, and renormalized solutions; the spaces themselves are on Sobolev spaces and spaces of time-dependent functions.

3. The energy structure

Both theorems rest on one identity. Define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy} E(t) = \int_\Omega \Big( \tfrac12 \rho |u|^2 + \tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\rho^\gamma + \tfrac{\chi D}{2a}|\nabla\phi|^2 + \tfrac{\chi b}{2a}\phi^2 - \chi\rho\phi \Big)\, dx . \end{equation}

routineThe energy identity (Lemma 2.1)

For smooth solutions, $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dt}E(t) = -\int_\Omega \Big( \tfrac{\chi\sigma}{a}|\phi_t|^2 + \alpha\rho|u|^2 + \mu|\nabla u|^2 + (\lambda+\mu)|\dv u|^2 \Big)dx \le 0.$

Test the momentum equation with $u$ and the $\phi$-equation with $\frac{\chi}{a}\phi_t$. The chemotactic work $\int \chi\rho\nabla\phi\cdot u$ is rewritten with the continuity equation as $\frac{d}{dt}\int\chi\rho\phi - \int\chi\rho\phi_t$, and the second piece cancels against the $\phi$-equation. Choosing the multipliers so that the cross terms cancel is the whole point.

Uses: energy estimates.

new ideaThe energy is bounded below for $\gamma > 4/3$ (Proposition 2.2)

Let $E_+$ be the energy without the cross term. Using only conservation of mass, $\int\rho = m$, one shows $\abs{\int\chi\rho\phi} \le C m^{q_0} + \frac12 E_+(t)$, so $E \ge \frac12 E_+ - Cm^{q_0}$. The cross term is split by Hölder, then $\norm{\rho}_{L^l}$ is interpolated between the conserved $L^1$ norm and the energy's $L^\gamma$ norm, and $\norm{\phi}_{L^{l'}}$ between $L^2$ and $H^1$. The exponents close (the power of $E_+$ stays below 1) exactly when $\gamma > 4/3$. Earlier work needed $\gamma > 8/5$. There are three cases, $\gamma > 2$, $\gamma = 2$, and $4/3 \lt \gamma \lt 2$; the last is where the exponent bookkeeping is delicate.

Uses: Hölder, Young, and Lᵖ interpolation, Gagliardo–Nirenberg, Sobolev embeddings.

4. Map of the proof of Theorem A

The strategy: first prove bounds that every smooth solution with $\rho_0 \ge \delta > 0$ must satisfy, with constants independent of $\delta$. Then build approximate solutions that satisfy the same bounds and pass to the limit. All the higher-order bounds are collected in one quantity,

\[ \Phi(t) = \sup_{0 \le s \le t}\Big( \norm{\rho(s)}_{H^1 \cap W^{1,q}} + \norm{\nabla u(s)}_{L^2} + \norm{\sqrt{\rho}\,u_t(s)}_{L^2} + \norm{\phi(s)}_{H^2} + 1 \Big), \]

and the goal of Section 3 of the paper is the inequality $\Phi(t) \le \exp\big[C\mathcal{M} + C\int_0^t M(\Phi(s))\,ds\big]$, where $\mathcal{M}$ depends only on the data (through $\norm{g}_{L^2}$) and $M$ is some increasing function. Such an inequality forces $\Phi$ to stay bounded for a short time.

routineStep 1. Second derivatives of $u$ from the equation (Lemma 3.2)

Read the momentum equation as $\Lame u = \rho u_t + \rho u\cdot\nabla u + \nabla p(\rho) - \chi\rho\nabla\phi + \alpha\rho u$ for the Lamé operator $\Lame$. Elliptic regularity bounds $\norm{\nabla u}_{H^1}$ by the right-hand side in $L^2$. The convection term produces $\norm{\nabla u}_{H^1}$ again, at a power below 2, so it is absorbed. Result: $\norm{\nabla u}_{H^1} \le M(\Phi)$.

Uses: elliptic regularity, Sobolev embeddings, Gagliardo–Nirenberg, Young's inequality.

routineStep 2. The time derivative $\sqrt\rho\, u_t$ (Lemmas 3.3–3.4)

Differentiate the momentum equation in $t$, test with $u_t$, and estimate the six resulting terms. The value at $t = 0$, $\norm{\sqrt{\rho_0}\,u_t(0)}_{L^2}$, is exactly $\norm{g}_{L^2}$ from the compatibility condition \eqref{eq:compat}. This is why that condition is needed: without it the estimate has no starting value when $\rho_0$ vanishes somewhere.

Uses: energy estimates, compatibility conditions, Sobolev embeddings.

routineStep 3. The density (Lemma 3.5)

The continuity equation is a transport equation: it carries $\rho$ along the flow and never smooths it. Its $W^{1,q}$ norm grows at most like $\exp\big(C\int_0^t \norm{\nabla u}_{H^1 \cap D^{1,q}}\big)$. So one needs $\nabla u \in L^1(0,T; W^{1,q})$ with $q > 3$, which comes from the $W^{2,q}$ estimate for the Lamé system and Step 2.

Uses: the transport equation, elliptic regularity.

routineStep 4. The chemical (Lemma 3.6)

Take the gradient of the $\phi$-equation, test with $\nabla\phi_t$, and use the Neumann condition. This bounds $\norm{\Delta\phi}_{L^2}$, hence $\norm{\phi}_{H^2}$ by elliptic regularity for the Neumann Laplacian.

Uses: parabolic regularity, elliptic regularity.

routineStep 5. Closing the estimate

Combine Steps 1–4 into the inequality for $\Phi$ above. A Gronwall-type continuity argument gives $T_0 > 0$, depending only on the data, with $\sup_{[0,T_0]}\Phi \le C\exp(C\norm{g}_{L^2})$.

Uses: Gronwall and continuity arguments.

routineStep 6. Construction (Section 4.1)

Lift the density to $\rho_0 + \delta$ so there is no vacuum, adjusting $u_0$ so that the compatibility condition still holds with the same $g$. Project $u$ onto finitely many eigenfunctions of the Lamé operator; solve the continuity and $\phi$ equations exactly and the projected momentum equation as an ODE system (the mass matrix is invertible because $\rho \ge \delta > 0$). The bounds of Step 5 hold uniformly in the number of modes and in $\delta$, so one can pass to weak limits, first in the number of modes and then $\delta \to 0$.

Uses: the Faedo–Galerkin method, weak and weak* convergence, spaces of time-dependent functions.

routineStep 7. Uniqueness (Section 4.2)

Subtract two solutions. Estimate the difference in the lower norm $\norm{\sqrt{\rho_1}\,u}_{L^2}^2 + \norm{\rho}_{L^2}^2 + \norm{\phi}_{H^1}^2$, using the regularity of the two solutions only as coefficients. The result is a linear Gronwall inequality with zero initial value, so the difference vanishes.

Uses: uniqueness by difference estimates, Gronwall's inequality.

5. Map of the proof of Theorem B

Weak solutions are built through a chain of regularized problems, each removing one difficulty, then the regularizations are removed one at a time. The paper concentrates on $3/2 \lt \gamma \lt 2$; larger $\gamma$ was covered by earlier work.

new ideaStep 1. The regularized system

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} &\rho_t + \dv(\rho u) = \varepsilon\Delta\rho, \\ &(\rho u)_t + \dv(\rho u\otimes u) + \nabla p(\rho) + \delta\nabla\rho^\beta + \varepsilon\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla u = \mu\Delta u + (\lambda+\mu)\nabla\dv u + \chi\rho\nabla\phi - \alpha\rho u, \\ &\sigma\phi_t + \varepsilon\Delta\big(|\Delta\phi|^{p_0}\Delta\phi\big) = D\Delta\phi + a\rho - b\phi, \qquad p_0 = \tfrac{2-\gamma}{\gamma-1}. \end{aligned} \right. \]

The first three regularizations are the standard Feireisl–Novotný–Petzeltová scheme: artificial viscosity $\varepsilon\Delta\rho$, a balancing term $\varepsilon\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla u$, and artificial pressure $\delta\rho^\beta$. The new difficulty: the artificial viscosity creates the term $-\varepsilon\int\phi\,\Delta\rho = -\varepsilon\int\rho\,\Delta\phi$ in the energy balance. Handled in the usual way, it leads to a term like $\varepsilon\int\rho^2$, which the energy controls only when $\gamma \ge 2$. The quasi-linear biharmonic term is designed for the range $3/2 \lt \gamma \lt 2$. It adds $\frac{\varepsilon\chi}{a(p_0+2)}\int|\Delta\phi|^{p_0+2}$ to the energy, and $p_0$ is chosen so that $p_0 + 2$ and $\gamma$ are conjugate exponents, so Young's inequality splits $\varepsilon\int\rho\,\Delta\phi$ between $\int\rho^\gamma$ and the new term.

Uses: regularized approximation schemes, Young's inequality.

routineStep 2. Uniform energy bounds (Lemma 5.1)

The energy identity of Section 3, redone for the regularized system, with the extra terms controlled as in Step 1 and the lower bound of Proposition 2.2. The constants do not depend on the Galerkin index, $\varepsilon$, or $\delta$.

Uses: energy estimates, Gronwall's inequality.

routineStep 3. Removing the regularizations

The Galerkin limit and $\varepsilon \to 0$ follow the compressible Navier–Stokes theory, using compactness to get strong convergence where the nonlinear terms need it. The biharmonic term disappears because the energy bounds $\varepsilon\int|\Delta\phi|^{p_0+2}$, so the term itself is $O(\varepsilon^{1/(p_0+2)})$ in the weak sense. The limit $\delta \to 0$ needs a little more integrability of $\rho$ than $L^\gamma$; this is where the pressure and the effective viscous flux enter.

Uses: compactness, Lions–Feireisl theory, weak convergence.

routineStep 4. Why $\gamma > 3/2$

For $\rho\nabla\phi$ to make sense in the weak formulation, $\nabla\phi$ must lie in $L^{\gamma'}$, $\gamma' = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}$, since $\rho$ is only in $L^\gamma$. Maximal regularity for the $\phi$-equation, with $a\rho \in L^\gamma$ as the source, gives $\phi \in W^{2,1}_\gamma$, so $\nabla\phi \in W^{1,\gamma}$ in space. The Sobolev embedding $W^{1,\gamma} \subset L^{3\gamma/(3-\gamma)}$ is enough when

\[ \frac{3\gamma}{3-\gamma} \gt \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1} \iff 3(\gamma - 1) \gt 3 - \gamma \iff \gamma \gt \tfrac32 . \]

The same threshold is where the Feireisl theory for compressible Navier–Stokes stops, so both obstructions meet at $3/2$.

Uses: maximal parabolic regularity, Sobolev embeddings.

6. Open questions

  • Vanishing damping. The construction of weak solutions never uses the damping term $\alpha\rho u$. What happens as $\alpha \to 0$?
  • Vanishing viscosity. Can weak solutions of the inviscid model ($\mu = \lambda = 0$) be obtained as limits of solutions of \eqref{eq:system}?
  • The gap $4/3 \lt \gamma \le 3/2$. The energy is bounded below there, but global weak solutions are not known to exist.
  • Lower dimensions. The same method should give weak solutions for $\gamma > 4/3$ in two dimensions and $\gamma > 1$ in one dimension.
