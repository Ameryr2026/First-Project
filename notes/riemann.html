Riemann Sums

Amer Rawashdeh · October 2026

To approximate the area under the graph of a function $f$ on an interval $[a,b]$, divide the interval into $n$ subintervals of equal width

\[ \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}, \]

choose a sample point $x_i^*$ in each subinterval, and add up the areas of the rectangles of height $f(x_i^*)$. The result is a Riemann sum:

\[ R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \]

Taking $f(x_i^*)$ to be the largest value of $f$ on each subinterval gives the upper sum $U_n$; the smallest value gives the lower sum $L_n$. Every Riemann sum lies between them, $L_n \le R_n \le U_n$, and the integral lies between them as well. Rectangles below the $x$-axis contribute negatively.

Try it

Enter a function, choose the bounds $a$ and $b$, and move the slider to change the number of rectangles $n$. The function can be typed as, for example, x^2 + 1, sin(x), e^x, sqrt(x), or 1/(1+x^2).

From sums to the integral

For a continuous function on $[a,b]$, the Riemann sums approach a single number as $n \to \infty$, whichever sample points are chosen. That number is the definite integral:

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]
